# Layered Character Design

Use this reference when creating or auditing `values`, `likes`, `dislikes`, `hopes`, `fears`, or the character's overall depth. Build a person whose different concerns can coexist, collide, and surprise without becoming random.

## Target result

Treat the concept and archetype as an entry point, not a master key that predicts every fact. Give the character several independent centers of attention: matters of conscience, private comfort, relationships, work, status, beauty, safety, belonging, identity, or the future can all pull in different directions.

For each of these fields, cover at least **three distinct thematic domains** and aim for **five** when the character is meant to carry substantial interaction:

- `values`
- `likes`
- `dislikes`
- `hopes`
- `fears`

Count domains, not phrasings. “Winning,” “being the best,” and “never appearing weak” may all belong to one status-and-competence domain. A separate domain introduces a concern with its own source, stakes, and possible behavior.

An intentionally single-minded character may concentrate these fields around one obsession. Record that concentration as a deliberate design choice in the concept, personality, or current situation, and show how it costs the character range. Otherwise, fail the depth audit and revise when a field has fewer than three domains or nearly every fact is predictable from the base archetype.

## Choose independent thematic domains

Select domains that matter to this particular character. This palette is a set of lenses, not a quota:

| Domain | Questions that create useful material |
| --- | --- |
| Inner identity | Who do they need to believe they are? What would cause shame or self-respect? |
| Relationships | Whose trust, affection, dependence, rivalry, or forgiveness matters? |
| Ethics and belief | What feels right, sacred, fair, forbidden, or worth a cost? |
| Work and competence | What do they enjoy mastering, making, fixing, teaching, or controlling? |
| Material life | How do money, shelter, possessions, debt, food, or security affect them? |
| Culture and belonging | Which community, tradition, class, place, faith, or subculture shaped them? |
| Sensory and aesthetic life | Which sounds, textures, tastes, spaces, clothes, art, or weather draw or repel them? |
| Status and power | Where do they seek recognition, independence, authority, privacy, or influence? |
| Body and safety | How do health, pain, touch, danger, aging, or physical freedom matter? |
| Future and existence | What legacy, change, meaning, mortality, freedom, or unknown do they face? |

Build with a mixture of consequential and ordinary domains. A small sensory pleasure or petty irritation can make the person feel inhabited; a moral belief or relational fear gives that texture stakes.

## Build the five depth fields

### Values and beliefs

Include principles from different origins and functions: an inherited rule, a chosen conviction, a private standard, a relational loyalty, or a belief tested by experience. Specify what each value makes the character choose when values compete. Let professed and practiced values diverge where the sheet supports hypocrisy, growth, or self-deception.

### Likes

Distribute enjoyment across activities, people or social dynamics, sensory preferences, ideas or aesthetics, rituals, objects, places, and forms of mastery. Give each preference a characteristic reason. “Likes tea” becomes more useful when the kind, occasion, attention, and emotional function reveal the person.

### Dislikes

Mix moral aversions, social irritants, sensory dislikes, threats to control or identity, reminders of history, and mundane annoyances. Define the response each one tends to produce: correction, withdrawal, argument, nausea, competition, mockery, vigilance, or another characteristic action.

### Hopes

Give the character desires at different distances and scales: an immediate improvement, a relationship outcome, a competence or work ambition, a private transformation, and a future or legacy. Some hopes may support each other; others should compete for time, loyalty, safety, or self-image.

### Fears

Combine concrete danger with relational loss, shame or identity collapse, moral failure, loss of agency, and a larger uncertainty where relevant. Tie each fear to a defense or overcompensation so it can shape decisions without requiring the character to announce it.

## Create layers through causality and tension

Use this workflow:

1. Write the core hook in one sentence.
2. Create a private matrix with the five fields as rows and candidate thematic domains as columns.
3. Fill at least three cells in every row; aim for five strong cells rather than five synonyms.
4. Give every entry a source: upbringing, experience, relationship, chosen identity, environment, body, vocation, culture, or present pressure.
5. Add at least two independent axes that a reader could not confidently predict from the archetype alone.
6. Add tensions with behavioral consequences: a value complicates a hope, a like exposes a fear, a dislike conflicts with a relationship, or two hopes demand incompatible choices.
7. Propagate facts selectively into motivations, relationships, modes, voice, and dialogue only where they actually change behavior.

Use causality to unify the person while preserving plurality. The same childhood may influence two fields differently, but every field does not need to echo it. Let separate parts of life remain separate until a situation makes them collide.

## Run the depth audit

Tag every entry in `values`, `likes`, `dislikes`, `hopes`, and `fears` with its thematic domain, then check:

- **Domain count:** every field has at least three distinct domains; major characters aim for five.
- **Root diversity:** the entries arise from several sources rather than one premise repeatedly reworded.
- **Independent axes:** at least two important traits, attachments, tastes, goals, or anxieties are not predictable from the archetype.
- **Tension:** at least two cross-field combinations can force a genuine trade-off or reveal contradiction.
- **Ordinary life:** the sheet contains specific pleasures, irritations, habits, or concerns that exist outside the central dramatic hook.
- **Selective influence:** major facts affect the sections they logically touch instead of being repeated everywhere.

Apply an archetype-swap test: temporarily replace the archetype with another familiar type. If most entries still read like direct variations of the original archetype's single defining idea, deepen the independent domains.

Pass only when the character can make plausible choices on several axes. If nearly everything in the sheet points back to one base trait, mark the design as intentionally single-minded or revise it.

## Revision moves

| Symptom | Positive revision move |
| --- | --- |
| Three entries that mean the same thing | keep the strongest one and add concerns from two genuinely different domains |
| Every field echoes the archetype | choose two life areas the archetype does not answer, then derive facts from concrete history and relationships |
| A list feels random | give each entry a causal source and connect selected pairs through tension or reinforcement |
| Only dramatic material appears | add an ordinary pleasure, irritation, ritual, practical concern, or aesthetic preference with a personal reason |
| Values read as virtues | include priorities that demand trade-offs and show what the character sacrifices for them |
| Hopes and fears mirror each other exactly | add desires and anxieties on different timescales and in different domains |
| Every fact appears in every section | keep one canonical home for each fact and propagate only its behavioral consequences |
